What is keratoconus?

Keratoconus is a progressive corneal ectasia: the clear front surface of the eye thins and steepens into a cone-like shape. That irregular curve produces myopia and astigmatism that often cannot be fully corrected with glasses. 1,2

It usually begins in adolescence or early adulthood and may keep changing into the thirties or forties. Both eyes are typically involved, but one is often worse. How common it is depends on the population and the test used—from older estimates of about 1 in 2,000 people to around 1 in 375 in a Dutch registry study, and higher in some Middle Eastern groups. 2–5,9

The cone adds higher-order aberrations—especially coma—that spectacles cannot cancel. Measured wavefront error in keratoconus can be several times that of a typical eye. People describe ghost copies, smear, glare, and washed contrast, which is what this page tries to show. 2,6,7

Strong risk factors include eye rubbing and a family history of keratoconus; allergy, asthma, and eczema also raise the odds. Care ranges from glasses, to rigid or scleral contact lenses, to corneal collagen cross-linking meant to slow progression. A minority of people later need a corneal transplant. 1,2,4,8

This simulator is an illustration, not a diagnosis. Vision in keratoconus is individual. Photos stay on this device.
